# Real Sheep Behaviour: Ethology Research for a Herding Simulation

**Purpose.** Reference notes for a game in which the mouse pointer acts as a predator/dog and the player herds a flock. Every numbered claim carries a source tag; the Sources list at the end gives URLs. Where only secondary (farm-education, popular) sources were available, this is flagged.

**Method note.** Web search summaries were the primary channel; direct page fetches were blocked for nearly all domains (grandin.com, sheep101.info, Wikipedia, PMC, Cell, Nature, bioRxiv etc.), so quotations are as reproduced in search snippets. Numbers from peer-reviewed work (King 2012, Strömbom 2014, Ginelli 2015, Jadhav 2024, Hutson, Michelena, Sibbald) should be treated as the most reliable; farm-blog speeds and vision figures are approximate.

---

## 1. Perception

### 1.1 Vision

| Quantity | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Total horizontal field of view | 270-320° (most sources say ~300°; humans ~180°) | [S1], [S2], [S3] |
| Binocular overlap (depth-capable zone, straight ahead) | ~30-40° (one source: 40°) | [S1], [S3] |
| Rear blind spot with head raised | ~70° directly behind the tail | [S3] |
| Front blind spot | Cannot see directly in front of the nose / immediately below the muzzle | [S4], [S1] |
| Head-down grazing | With the head down, the horizontal pupil lets the sheep see all round; pupils counter-rotate ~50° to stay horizontal as the head lowers | [S1], [S3] |
| Depth perception | Poor; horizontal pupil trades distance/speed judgement for panoramic coverage | [S1], [S2] |

Behavioural consequences (handling literature):

- Sheep **stop and hesitate at shadows, puddles, shiny metal, drains, abrupt floor changes, flapping clothing, and people leaning over a race**, because they cannot judge depth well and must lower/turn the head to bring a thing into the binocular zone before committing. [S5], [S6]
- They **hesitate when moving from bright to dark**, and prefer to move from dim toward brighter areas provided the light is not glaring in their eyes. [S3], [S6]
- After **detecting a stimulus peripherally, the sheep turns its head to examine it with binocular vision** ("locate, then look"), which is why an alert sheep stands square, head up, facing the threat. [S7]
- Because the blind spot is behind, a handler standing **directly behind a sheep loses influence**: the animal will turn its head or body to bring the handler back into view, which is one of the reasons livestock circle a handler (see §2). Grandin's point of balance is explicitly "determined by the animal's wide angle vision". [S8]
- Sheep have good visual acuity for recognising individuals (faces, both sheep and human) and remember aversive handling; Hutson notes "sheep have good memories for aversive procedures". [S9]

### 1.2 Hearing and smell

- Hearing range reported as **125 Hz to 42 kHz** (i.e. into ultrasound), sensitivity ~10 dB; ears rotate ~180° independently of one another and act as directional filters. [S10]
- Sheep are especially wary of **loud, sudden, high-pitched noises**; the first instinct is to move away from the sound's origin. Quiet handling is recommended for this reason, and ultrasound from machinery is a suspected stressor. [S10], [S11]
- Smell is well developed: ewes recognise lambs by odour, and sheep "rely heavily on sounds and smells to determine whether there may be a threat". [S11], [S12]
- **Novel sounds do not habituate quickly**: a PLOS One study found a lack of habituation to repeated novel sounds. [S13]

### 1.3 Keeping the handler in view

- Sheep want to keep the threat inside the wide lateral field but out of the rear blind spot. When the handler is out of sight they become uneasy and turn. In a single-file race they "must be able to see the animal ahead" and will refuse to move if they see people up ahead. [S8], [S14]
- Sheep are "harder to move when isolated, rushed, shouted at, or forced toward a dark corner" and easier "when they can see or rejoin other sheep". [S6]

## 2. Flight zone, pressure and release

### 2.1 Flight zone size

| Situation | Approximate radius | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Completely tame / pet sheep | 0 m (can be touched; must be led, not driven) | [S8], [S15] |
| Merino ewes in a 2 m-wide laneway (Hutson) | 5.7 m | [S16] |
| Merino ewes in a 4 m-wide laneway (Hutson) | 11.4 m | [S16] |
| Extensively raised (range) cattle, for scale | up to 50 m | [S17] |
| Feedlot cattle, for scale | 1.5-8 m | [S17] |
| Sheep in confined space vs open paddock | Smaller in confinement, larger in open space | [S15] |
| Flock in open field approached by a dog (King 2012) | Flock began to move at ~70 m | [S18] |

Modifiers (all directions consistent across Grandin, Hutson, extension guides):

- **Tameness / handling frequency**: frequent gentle handling shrinks the zone; unhandled range sheep have a large zone. [S8], [S15]
- **Approach angle**: the zone is **larger for a head-on approach** than an angled one; recommended working position is at the edge of the zone, **45-60° behind the shoulder**. [S19]
- **Speed and directness**: "an approaching figure that is large, fast, or moving directly toward the animal triggers a bigger flight response than one that is small, slow, or angled"; sudden movements enlarge the zone. [S19]
- **Predator vs human**: sheep show more aversion to a dog than to a human in arena tests; dogs in the yards raise cortisol. [S20], [S9]
- **Enclosure width**: Hutson's laneway data (5.7 m at 2 m width vs 11.4 m at 4 m width) shows the flight distance scales with the space available to flee. [S16]

### 2.2 Point of balance

- The **point of balance is at the shoulder** when the handler is close; from further away it shifts forward "to just behind the eye". Handler **behind** the point of balance → animal moves **forward**; handler **in front** → animal **stops/backs up**. [S8], [S21]
- Walking **past the animals in the opposite direction** to the desired travel (crossing each animal's point of balance from front to back) pulls a line of animals forward. [S8]
- When approached from the front the sheep will not walk into the handler's hand; it backs away. [S22]

### 2.3 Pressure / release

- Handlers apply pressure by stepping into the edge of the zone and **release it by stepping out**; "whatever pressure is applied must be released" (move toward, then away). Continuous pressure produces panic rather than steady flow. [S22]
- **Penetrating too deep into the flight zone** causes animals to bolt, turn back, or climb fences; Grandin's remedy is to back out of the zone. In a narrow chute the handler should stay **~10 ft (3 m) back from the last sheep** to stop rear animals turning and running past. [S8], [S22]
- Once excited, **sheep need 20-30 minutes to calm down** (Grandin); heart rate alone returns to near-normal in 5-10 min after mild handling, but after dog-driving it was still elevated 2 hours later, and cortisol after shearing needed ~90 min to reach baseline. [S8], [S23], [S24]

### 2.4 Why sheep circle the handler

- With a lateral, near-panoramic field and a rear blind spot, the animal's comfortable flight direction is **away-and-around**, keeping the handler in the side field rather than dead behind. In a round tub or pen, "the cattle will circle around a person standing at the gate pivot"; sheep behave the same, which is why round forcing pens and curved races work. [S8], [S21]
- A handler who follows a fleeing animal straight-line will find it curving; a handler who instead moves laterally across the point of balance controls direction. [S8]

## 3. Flocking instinct

### 3.1 Gregariousness and minimum group size

| Fact | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Flocking is observed in groups as small as | 4 individuals | [S25] |
| Practical minimum husbandry group | 2, better 3-5 | [S26] |
| Isolation | "Highly stressful"; high-pitched bleating, vigilance, raised heart rate, respiration and cortisol; feeding/ruminating/lying time collapses | [S9], [S27] |
| Visual isolation for 23 h | Heart rate up after 0.5 h; cortisol still elevated at 23 h | [S27] |

- Sheep "synchronize their behavior with other sheep" and "perform allelomimetic behavior by mimicking the actions of other sheep in the flock". [S7]
- Merck: "Sheep have such intense following behavior that they can be moved in a more continuous flow" than cattle. [S21]

### 3.2 Followers, leaders and lamb-ewe bonds

- "Sheep will follow a leader. If you can get one sheep moving, then the rest will most likely follow." Leader sheep are sometimes kept deliberately to ease handling. [S6], [S28]
- Leadership is **fluid rather than fixed**: Nature Physics (2022) found each motion episode has a temporary leader in a line formation and that roles alternate between episodes; older/bolder ewes are over-represented at the front but not exclusively. Observers also describe consistent "Leaders", "Middlers" and "Tailers". [S29]
- During dog-driving, **directional information propagates from front to rear** even though the dog pushes from behind; front-positioned sheep have the largest influence on collective direction (Jadhav 2024). [S30]
- **Ewe-lamb bond**: forms in the first 30 min after birth (sensitive period ~4 h); lambs recognise the dam's low-pitched bleat by 12 h; ewes stay close and "encourage following behaviour, which young lambs learn quickly"; ewe and lambs rejoin the flock within a few days. [S12]
- Ewes with young lambs are the exception to "flee not fight": they stamp, lower the head and may butt a dog or fox. [S31]

### 3.3 Breed differences

- **Merino**: strongly flocking, "only sub-group when they are young or during high temperatures and during times of food shortages"; calm, suited to large mobs. [S32]
- **Hill breeds (Scottish Blackface, Welsh Mountain)**: "tend to remain dispersed unless disturbed"; they hold home ranges ("hefts") on open hill. [S32]
- Merinos and Corriedales both spread further apart as flock size increases; Merinos also close up as density falls. [S33]
- Nearest-neighbour distance varies by breed and also by vegetation type and space allowance. [S34]

### 3.4 Spacing, bunching and the selfish herd

| Context | Distance / metric | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Nearest-neighbour, grazing same patch | 4.9 m mean | [S34] |
| Nearest-neighbour, grazing different patches | 13.4 m | [S34] |
| Nearest-neighbour on heather | 9.6 m | [S34] |
| Sub-groups of 2-3 sheep on extensive grassland | 10-200 m from other sub-groups | [S35] |
| Merinos on saltbush | graze as one flock or sub-flocks of >300 | [S35] |
| Larger groups | mean inter-individual distance constant, nearest-neighbour distance decreases | [S34] |
| Shy vs bold individuals | shy groups pack tighter and close up more readily when disturbed | [S36] |
| Dog approaches within ~70 m (King 2012, 46 sheep, Kelpie) | every sheep moves toward the flock centroid; outermost sheep travel furthest; a tight cluster forms | [S18] |

- King et al. (2012) gave the first hard test of Hamilton's selfish-herd theory: sheep run **toward the centre of the flock, not directly away from the dog**, with attraction to the centroid switching on when the dog is within a threshold radius. [S18], [S37]
- In domestic sheep "behavioral responses to predation risk are closely related to flock size and include bunching, increases in attentiveness, and fleeing". [S36]

## 4. Danger response sequence

Assembled from Merck, the *Animal Welfare* review "How has the risk of predation shaped the behavioural responses of sheep", sanctuary/farm guides and the arena tests:

1. **Detection** (peripheral). Head comes up, ears forward and locked on, body turns to bring the stimulus into the 30-40° binocular zone. "Scanning" is defined in vigilance studies as an immobile posture with the head raised above the spine line. [S7], [S38]
2. **Freeze and stare**, often several seconds, while the rest of the group notices the posture change (visual contagion). Ear and tail position change more frequently in negative states. [S7], [S31]
3. **Alarm signals**: snort, alarm bleat, stepping toward the stimulus, **foot-stamping**, head-bowing, and stiff-legged bouncing. These act as "pursuit-deterrence" signals ("I have seen you") and are used against coyotes. [S39], [S40]
4. **Bunching** (selfish herd) begins before flight: every sheep moves toward the centroid; the flock contracts. [S18]
5. **Flight**: "running away a short distance to form a flock and then turning round to face the predator." The flock runs as one, stops together, and **faces the threat**. [S31]
6. **Vigilance while regrouping**; grazing resumes gradually. Group-size effect: larger groups spend less time per animal vigilant. [S38]
7. **Recovery**: 20-30 min to calm after real excitement (Grandin); 5-10 min heart-rate recovery after mild handling; hours after dog-driving. [S8], [S23]

Contagion and waves:

- Regrouping in Ginelli et al.'s Merino flocks is **avalanche-like**: "heightened fear eventually triggers an individual to suddenly move closer to its neighbors, and regrouping events appear to be strongly allelomimetic in that they trigger the same regrouping behavior in neighboring sheep", producing fast collective contractions at all observed scales. [S41]
- Spontaneous group walks propagate as a **travelling pulse** through the flock even though each sheep is individually either stopped or walking; the pulse has constant speed and a sech-squared shape (Azaïs/Toulet/Bon/Gautrais). [S42]
- Individuals "increase their own vigilance in response to the alertness or escape behaviour of others" (social transmission of stress). [S43]

Habituation:

- Sheep habituate to repeated **non-threatening** stimuli in approach-avoidance tests (attention to a previously seen dog wall declines across trials), but **novel sounds** showed a lack of habituation, and sheep in an anxious state stay vigilant longer, feed less and keep looking at the place where the dog was. [S13], [S44]

## 5. Locomotion

### 5.1 Gaits and speeds

| Mode | Speed | Notes / source |
|---|---|---|
| Grazing drift | ~0 m/s with short steps; sheep alternate "grazing mode at null speed and moving mode at walking speed" | [S42] |
| Walking between patches | 2.2-2.7 km/h (0.6-0.75 m/s) for Border Leicester and Merino; 0.8 km/h for Dorset Horn; 60-78 m/min (1.0-1.3 m/s) in paddocks | [S45] |
| Trot (driven, unhurried) | roughly 2-4 m/s (inferred; no peer-reviewed figure found) | — |
| Gallop / bound (fleeing) | 16-24 km/h (4.5-6.7 m/s) typical burst; top 32-40 km/h (9-11 m/s); one claim of 45 km/h for Suffolk | [S46], [S47] |
| Sustained running | Not built for it; top speed "only in short bursts" when threatened | [S47] |
| Daily distance | 8-16 km/day free-grazing (14 km Border Leicester, 13.7 km Merino, 9 km Dorset Horn; 9.6 km mountain pasture) | [S45] |
| Border Collie for comparison | 20-30 mph (9-13 m/s) top | [S48] |
| Shepherding-model speed ratio | dog 1.25-1.5x sheep speed is enough to control the flock | [S49] |

Speed sources for sheep are farm-education sites, not biomechanics papers; treat 6-7 m/s burst and ~10 m/s absolute maximum as reasonable design values.

### 5.2 Lambs

- Lambs **pronk/stot** (all four feet leave the ground, stiff-legged, back arched) as locomotor play, frequently in the evening and in group "gambolling" races along fences; adults stot only rarely as an alarm/fitness signal. [S50]

### 5.3 Group movement when driven

- **Line and wedge**: spontaneous movements happen in **line formation** behind a temporary leader; large flocks adopt "the same follow-my-leader formation, albeit in multiple lines at once" — i.e. an elongated body with a narrow head and a broad tail (wedge). [S29]
- Directional information flows **front to back**; the front sheep decide, the rear follow, and the dog's turning rate leads the flock barycentre's turning rate. [S30]
- Sheep prefer to **move uphill**, **toward light**, **away from buildings**, **toward other sheep**, **around gentle curves** rather than sharp corners, and **will not move toward what looks like a dead end**. [S6], [S5]
- **Balking** happens at shadows, drains, changes of flooring, people ahead in the race, and partially blocked views; a curved race works because it hides people and dead ends. [S5], [S14]
- Through a gap the flock **funnels**: the first sheep hesitates, then once one is through the rest pour after it ("if you can get one sheep moving, the rest will follow"). Each animal must keep sight of the one ahead. [S6], [S14]
- **Breaking back**: over-pressured sheep in a race or at a gate "turn around and run past you"; the fix is to stand ~3 m back, release pressure, and not push the tail-enders. Young dogs that crowd the flock "very often" cause it to **split**, and are drawn to chase the individuals rather than the group. [S22], [S51]
- In a big flock a dog can enter the middle: "the sheep retreat but don't scatter. Then the dog can push the flock forward from within, like a driver in a car." [S29]

## 6. Daily behaviour

| Activity | Time budget | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Grazing | 6-8 h/day typical; mean 8 h, upper limit 12-13 h; in 5-6 bouts of 20-90 min | [S52], [S53] |
| Rumination | ~2 h lying + ~1 h standing per day; bouts of 14 min (lying) / 9 min (standing) every 2-3 h | [S54] |
| Resting/idling | 45-90 min rests between grazing bouts; daylight idle ~3.2 h | [S53], [S55] |
| Sleep | ~4-5 h per 24 h, mostly at night, light sleep while ruminating | [S54] |
| Daylight split on pasture | 60-70% feeding, 20-30% resting, 5-10% walking, ~5% social/drinking/grooming | [S55] |
| Diel pattern | Bimodal grazing peaks at dawn and late afternoon; midday and night quiet; in winter the main meal is midday | [S45], [S52] |

Spatial dynamics:

- **Synchronised activity**: the flock grazes together and rests together; feeding is socially facilitated (Michelena, Gautrais et al.). [S56]
- **Expansion/contraction cycles**: while grazing, sheep balance maximising individual foraging space against the safety of a dense group; the result is slow spreading punctuated by fast, avalanche-like regroupings, with the strength of allelomimesis setting how far the group can spread before snapping back (Ginelli 2015, ~100 Merinos). [S41]
- **Relocation triggers**: a few individuals raise heads and begin walking; once enough neighbours ahead are walking, others switch from grazing to walking (switching rates depend on the states of sheep ahead vs behind), so the whole group moves as a travelling pulse to a new patch, then stops together. [S42]
- Relaxed flocks on good pasture keep ~5 m nearest-neighbour spacing on a shared patch and up to 13 m across patches; sub-groups of 2-3 may sit 10-200 m apart on extensive ground. [S34], [S35]
- Sheep use a small fraction of a large paddock intensively; dominant animals occupy preferred areas. [S35]

## 7. Sheepdog work

### 7.1 Elements of a run

| Element | Description | Numbers | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outrun | Dog leaves handler's side in a wide **pear-shaped** arc to get behind the sheep without disturbing them | 350-500 yd (320-460 m) in Open trials | [S57], [S58] |
| Lift | First contact at the top; dog walks up quietly, sheep start moving toward handler | Judged on calm, straight start | [S57] |
| Fetch | Bringing sheep in a straight line to the handler, through fetch gates | 20 points of 100 | [S59] |
| Drive | Pushing sheep **away** from the handler through drive gates and a cross-drive | 30 points | [S59] |
| Shed / single | Separating specified sheep from the group | 10 points | [S59] |
| Pen | Putting all sheep into a 6 x 9 ft (1.8 x 2.7 m) pen | 10 points | [S59] |
| Time limit | Whole course | ~12 min | [S59] |
| Sheep per run | 3-5 | [S60] |
| Farm work | Hundreds; hill gathers may bring several hundred off open ground | [S57] |

### 7.2 How the dog controls the flock

- **Eye**: the fixed stare and crouched stalk, an amplified predatory approach with the grab-bite bred out; it "exerts pressure on the sheep from a distance" purely psychologically. [S61]
- **Balance**: the dog positions itself on the opposite side of the flock from where the handler wants it to go, at the flock's point of balance; moving left/right ("flanking") steers, moving in ("walk up") pushes. A flank "should not make the stock move" — it repositions the dog at constant distance. [S51], [S62]
- **Wearing**: for large mobs the dog sweeps side to side behind the flock to keep the tail from spreading and to keep the whole bunch together. [S51]
- **Why the curve works**: a straight, direct approach is read as attack (larger flight zone, §2.1), while a wide curve keeps the dog outside the zone until it is exactly behind the sheep relative to the handler, so the lift is a controlled, straight push rather than a scatter. "Towards the end of the outrun, the dog should move in behind the sheep, close enough to gain control, but leaving enough room to avoid disturbing them." [S57], [S19]
- **Gathering vs driving** (Strömbom et al. 2014): a single dog can herd >100 sheep with two rules — **collect** the furthest-out sheep when any animal is more than f(N) from the flock's centre of mass, otherwise **drive** from a position directly behind the flock relative to the target. In the model sheep sense the dog at 65 units vs 2 units for neighbours, use n nearest neighbours (0.5-0.75 N works), and are repelled by the dog with weight 1 vs attraction 1.05 to local centre and repulsion 2 from close neighbours; the dog stops when within 3 body-radii of a sheep. [S49], [S63]
- **Too close**: over-pressuring "very often" splits the group; splits produce breakaways that the dog then has to re-collect, and young dogs fixate on the individuals. Good dogs "give the sheep space" and keep the same distance all the way round. [S51]
- **Empirical driving data** (Jadhav et al. 2024, 14 Merino ewes, one Border Collie, UWB tags): the dog stays behind the flock relative to its heading; the flock's heading alternates as the dog swings from side to side; front sheep lead direction; the agent-based model reproducing this used a dog-repulsion radius R_D = 3 (model units) for N = 14. [S30]
- **Field response distance**: 46 sheep began moving when a Kelpie came within ~70 m, then contracted toward the centroid. [S18]

## 8. Implications for a game simulation

Rules a flock model should obey to read as real sheep, each traceable to the evidence above:

1. **Per-sheep perception is a 300° lateral fan with a ~70° rear blind cone and a 30-40° frontal binocular cone.** A pointer/dog inside the rear cone should not be "seen" until the sheep turns or a neighbour reacts; a threat seen peripherally should make the sheep pivot to face it before deciding to flee. (§1.1)
2. **Flight zone radius is a per-sheep, per-context number, not a constant.** Baseline 5-12 m for handled sheep (Hutson), scaled up by: head-on approach (x1.5-2), fast approach, unfamiliar or dog-like stimulus, open space, and by the individual's timidity; scaled down by confinement and habituation. A whole flock in open field reacts at ~70 m to a dog. (§2.1)
3. **Point of balance at the shoulder decides direction.** Pressure behind the shoulder line → move forward/away; pressure ahead of it → stop, back up, or turn. Crossing the line from front to back should produce a step forward. (§2.2)
4. **Pressure that is not released causes panic.** Sustained penetration of the flight zone should ramp an "excitement" state that increases speed, tightens bunching, and makes splits/breakbacks likely; stepping out of the zone should decay it slowly (minutes, not seconds; 20-30 min to fully calm). (§2.3)
5. **Sheep flee toward the flock centre first, and away from the threat second.** Model the escape vector as w1·(toward centroid) + w2·(away from dog), with w1 dominating as the dog gets closer (selfish herd, King 2012). Outer sheep move furthest and fastest. (§3.4)
6. **Bunching tightness scales with fear.** Relaxed grazing spacing ~5 m; alarmed spacing ~1 body length. Contraction happens as an avalanche (a few sheep move, neighbours copy within a second), expansion is slow and gradual. (§3.4, §6)
7. **Everything is allelomimetic.** One sheep switching state (graze→alert, alert→flee, stop→walk) raises the probability that neighbours switch; the response should propagate as a visible wave through the flock, front-to-back for direction, outside-in for bunching. (§4, §6)
8. **Alarm has a staged sequence with timing**: head-up freeze and stare (1-5 s) → stamp/snort (optional, more likely from ewes with lambs or bold individuals) → bunch → run a short distance → stop as a group and turn to face the threat → resume grazing gradually. Skipping straight from grazing to sprinting looks wrong. (§4)
9. **Isolation is aversive.** A sheep separated from all others (no neighbour within, say, 15-20 m or line of sight) should bleat, raise vigilance, stop grazing and actively seek the flock, even running past the dog to rejoin it. Groups of fewer than ~4 should be visibly uneasy. (§3.1)
10. **Following beats pathfinding.** Individuals mostly steer toward the sheep ahead of them; only the front few sheep choose the route. This produces line/wedge shapes when moving, natural funnelling through gaps (hesitate, then pour), and the "one goes, all go" effect. (§5.3)
11. **Leadership is soft and rotating.** Bolder/older ewes should have a higher probability of initiating moves, but any sheep at the front can lead a given episode. (§3.2)
12. **Environmental biases**: prefer uphill, prefer light over dark, avoid buildings, avoid apparent dead ends, balk at shadows/ground-texture changes, move more readily around gentle curves than sharp corners, and refuse to move toward a handler standing in the intended path. (§5.3)
13. **Speeds**: walk 0.6-1.3 m/s, driven trot ~2-4 m/s, flight 5-7 m/s, absolute cap ~10 m/s, with a stamina budget so that sprinting lasts seconds and degrades to a trot. The dog/pointer should be only ~1.25-1.5x faster than sheep for the herding to feel like real sheepdog work. (§5.1, §7.2)
14. **Too much pressure splits the flock.** When the dog comes within the flock's inner zone or pushes the tail faster than the head can lead, model a split: a sub-group breaks off at an angle, and individuals at the rear may break back past the dog. Recollecting them is the player's cost. (§7.2)
15. **The curve is rewarded.** A pointer approaching in a wide arc should slip behind the flock without triggering flight; a straight approach should trigger flight at a greater distance and in a less predictable direction. Reward the player for reaching the balance point quietly. (§7.2, §2.1)
16. **Collect-then-drive is the correct controller behaviour and a good AI dog rule.** If any sheep is further than f(N) ≈ r_a·N^(2/3) from the centroid, target the straggler; otherwise drive from behind the centroid relative to the goal. (§7.2)
17. **Daily rhythm as a background state machine.** Long grazing bouts (20-90 min), rest/ruminate bouts (45-90 min), synchronised across the flock, with a slow spreading drift during grazing and periodic spontaneous relocations as travelling pulses. The pointer's arrival interrupts this and the flock takes many minutes to return to it. (§6)
18. **Breed as a tuning preset**: Merino-type = strong cohesion, small spacing, rarely sub-groups; hill-breed type = larger spacing, forms sub-groups 10-200 m apart, disperses unless disturbed. (§3.3)
19. **Lambs add noise**: they pronk in play, lag then sprint to catch up, stick within a few metres of the dam, and the dam turns to stamp at a threat instead of fleeing cleanly. (§3.2, §5.2)
20. **Habituation, but not to novelty or sound.** Repeated harmless presence of the pointer at the zone edge should slowly shrink the flight zone; sudden sound or a new object should reset vigilance. (§4)
