Sheepdog Trial

Your pointer is the dog: nothing is drawn for it, the sheep simply treat wherever it is as the thing to keep away from. Twenty-four ewes graze, drift and bunch by rules taken from GPS studies of real flocks. Move into their flight zone and they will move away from you, but through their own centre first, the way sheep actually do.

Move the pointer onto the paddock to take the dog. Approach slowly and wide; come in fast and straight and you will scatter them. Scroll to zoom, or press 0 to see the whole field.

Work the flight zone

Each ewe carries her own flight zone, about 3 body lengths for a still pointer and 8 for a moving one, widened by nerves and shrunk by boldness. Pressure at the edge moves them. Pressure released lets them settle.

Mind the point of balance

Press behind a ewe’s shoulder and she goes forward; press ahead of it and she stops or turns back. To send the flock left, be on its right.

Alarm travels

Fright spreads by sight from neighbour to neighbour, weaker at each hop, so one startled ewe usually ripples and dies out while a real threat sweeps the whole flock.

Sheep see about 300 degrees. Come at them from the blind cone behind and they notice late, then startle rather than easing away.

Do not dive in

Push into the middle and the flock fractures, with tail-enders breaking back past you. Stragglers must be collected before the rest can be driven, which is what a real outrun is for.

The tuning panel, on the button or the d key, exposes every behavioural parameter live: flight zones, fear thresholds, contagion, habituation, cohesion, gaits and the collision solver, with a filter box and a button that copies just what you changed. Zoom with the wheel, the slider, or the + and - keys; 0 returns to the whole paddock. Once you are in close the camera follows the flock, so a herd being driven stays on screen. Flock size runs to 500. Past roughly 150 the sheep stop casting real shadows and start animating in rotation rather than every frame, which is what keeps a big flock affordable; the paddock also grows with the flock so the grazing density stays realistic. Behaviour follows the published field work: intermittent grazing and avalanche re-packing (Ginelli et al. 2015), running to the flock centre rather than away from the dog (King et al. 2012), the shepherd’s repulsion and local-centre attraction model (Strömbom et al. 2014), rotating temporary leaders in walking lines (Gómez-Nava et al. 2022), and driven-flock spacing and polarisation (Jadhav et al. 2024). Distances are in sheep body lengths; outer ranges are compressed to fit a paddock on a screen. This page is the simulation on its own. It is destined for a transparent always-on-top window where the flock grazes across your desktop.
